NISAR stands for NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar. It uses Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) to observe Earth's surface. Unlike optical satellites, SAR collects useful images at night and through cloud, smoke and many weather conditions.

The mission helps scientists monitor earthquakes, landslides, volcanoes, floods, glaciers, forests, agriculture, wetlands, sea ice and ground deformation.

Main goal: measure changes in Earth's land, water, vegetation and ice to understand natural hazards, climate change, ecosystems and human impact on the planet.

Why NISAR matters

  • Detects very small movements in Earth's surface.
  • Works through clouds and at night.
  • Monitors forests, glaciers, agriculture and wetlands.
  • Helps track earthquakes, landslides, volcanoes and floods.
  • Supports disaster response and climate research.
  • Data are free for research and education.

First images

NISAR's first radar images

Both first images were captured by the L-band radar and released by NASA and ISRO. The official pictures are hosted on NASA's site and open in a new tab.

Mount Desert Island, Maine

L-bandCaptured 21 Aug 2025

Illustration of the scene in NISAR's colours, not NISAR data. The real image is on NASA's site.

Water appears dark, forest appears green, and hard surfaces such as bare ground and buildings appear magenta. The town of Bar Harbor shows as a magenta patch at the island's northeast end, with narrow waterways and small islets around it.

View official image

Forest River, North Dakota

L-bandCaptured 23 Aug 2025

Illustration of the scene in NISAR's colours, not NISAR data. The real image is on NASA's site.

The area straddles Grand Forks and Walsh counties. Forests and wetlands line the river, with farmland to the north and south. Dark plots are likely fallow fields, lighter plots likely pasture or crops such as soybean and corn. Circular patterns show centre-pivot irrigation.

Radar

SAR data

SAR means Synthetic Aperture Radar, an imaging method that uses microwaves to build detailed images of Earth's surface.

A SAR satellite sends radar signals toward Earth and measures the reflection. That lets it observe the surface through clouds, darkness, smoke and some weather.

Radar bands compared

Longer wavelengths penetrate vegetation more deeply and stay coherent over longer periods. Bars are drawn to scale.

SAR applications

  • Earthquakes: ground movement after an event.
  • Landslides: unstable slopes and displacement.
  • Volcanoes: deformation near vents.
  • Floods: water-covered areas from radar backscatter.
  • Forests: structure, biomass, deforestation.
  • Agriculture: crop and soil-moisture studies.
  • Glaciers: movement and ice loss.
  • Sea ice: extent and movement.
  • Urban subsidence: sinking land in cities.
  • Wetlands: mapping and seasonal water change.

Interferogram simulator

One colour cycle (fringe) means the ground moved half a wavelength along the radar line of sight. Move the slider to simulate a bulge or subsidence bowl and compare the three bands.

6.0 cm

Fringes = movement / (wavelength / 2). Idealised model with a smooth bowl, no noise or atmosphere. Real interferograms also lose coherence over vegetation, more so at short wavelengths.

Orbit and coverage calculator

Change the altitude and swath width to see how the orbit sets the 12-day coverage. The defaults are NISAR's reported altitude and swath. Ascending passes only; descending passes give a second set.

Terrain distortion explorer

A side-looking radar measures distance along its line of sight, so hills distort. Set the radar incidence angle and the hill slope to see foreshortening, layover and shadow. This is why terrain-corrected (RTC) products exist.

Facing slope: layover when slope > incidence angle, otherwise squeezed by sin(incidence − slope) / sin(incidence). Far slope: shadow when slope > 90° − incidence angle. Idealised flat geometry, no Earth curvature.
